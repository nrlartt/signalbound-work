# Condorcet's jury theorem and the wisdom of crowds

## Observations

OBSERVATION: Condorcet's jury theorem is a statistical theorem about the probability of a group arriving at a correct decision by majority vote (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet%27s_jury_theorem).

OBSERVATION: The theorem's assumptions are that a group reaches a decision by majority vote, one of the two outcomes is correct, and each voter has an independent probability p of voting for the correct decision (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet%27s_jury_theorem).

OBSERVATION: If p is greater than 1/2, adding more voters increases the probability that the majority decision is correct, and in the limit that probability approaches 1 as the number of voters increases (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet%27s_jury_theorem).

OBSERVATION: If p is less than 1/2, adding more voters makes things worse, and the optimal jury consists of a single voter (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet%27s_jury_theorem).

OBSERVATION: The theorem was first expressed by the Marquis de Condorcet in his 1785 work Essay on the Application of Analysis to the Probability of Majority Decisions (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet%27s_jury_theorem).

OBSERVATION: Wisdom of the crowd expresses the notion that the collective opinion of a diverse and independent group of individuals yields the best judgement rather than that of a single expert (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom_of_the_crowd).

OBSERVATION: Jury theorems from social choice theory provide formal arguments for wisdom of the crowd given a variety of more or less plausible assumptions, and both the assumptions and conclusions remain controversial (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom_of_the_crowd).

OBSERVATION: The oldest and simplest jury theorem is Condorcet's jury theorem (1785) (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom_of_the_crowd).

OBSERVATION: Galton's classic wisdom-of-the-crowds finding involved point estimation of a quantity at a cattle show, where a crowd estimated the weight of an ox (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom_of_the_crowd).

OBSERVATION: The median estimate of the ox's weight was 1,207 pounds, within 1% of the true weight of 1,198 pounds (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom_of_the_crowd).

OBSERVATION: The Condorcet page states that since Condorcet many researchers have proved other jury theorems relaxing some or all of Condorcet's assumptions, implying the original assumptions can fail, but it does not name correlated voters or any specific violated assumption in the fetched excerpt (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet%27s_jury_theorem).

## Inferences

INFERENCE: Condorcet's jury theorem supports the wisdom of crowds only when voters are independent and individually better than random (p > 1/2); under those conditions larger groups converge on correctness, while if p < 1/2 larger groups worsen the decision (source: OBSERVATION lines about the theorem's conclusions and about wisdom of the crowd drawing on jury theorems).

INFERENCE: Galton's ox result illustrates the wisdom of crowds because the crowd's median estimate of 1,207 pounds was close to the true weight of 1,198 pounds (source: OBSERVATION lines about Galton's example).
